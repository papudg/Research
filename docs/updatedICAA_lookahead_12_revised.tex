\begin{abstract}
Sequential examination-paper assembly can be formulated as Boolean constraint satisfaction under structural, temporal, chapter-coverage, and non-duplication requirements. We present an applied algorithm-engineering study of policy design for a production-derived metadata-only setting, rather than a new CP-SAT algorithm. Beyond a first-feasible solver, we evaluate an independent constructive--local baseline, weighted and direct-compliance single objectives, sequential lexicographic optimization, and a joint whole-paper lexicographic control on a 475-item CBSE Class X Mathematics corpus. The contribution is a reproducible policy comparison and application benchmark, not a new CP-SAT solving algorithm. Across five independently generated 80-blueprint manifests, sequential $B1$ has seed-level median difficulty deviation 38--40 versus 50--52 for first-feasible $B0$; the direct-compliance control and weighted policy achieve equal difficulty with lower Bloom deviation and solver runtime. The five-manifest clustered paired analysis is treated as the primary robustness evidence, with pooled blueprint counts used only descriptively. On the fixed manifest, joint and sequential lexicographic policies tie on all 70 comparable cases, and four section orders have identical aggregate medians. Results concern structural alignment and solver runtime only, not psychometric validity, end-to-end latency, or universal policy dominance. The complete fixture, manifests, code, environment, and result files are supplied for regeneration.
\keywords{Constraint Satisfaction Problem \and Multi-Objective Optimization \and Automated Test Assembly \and CP-SAT \and Lexicographic Optimization \and Educational Assessment.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%===============================================================================

Large-scale educational assessment platforms manage repositories containing tens of thousands of test items. Paper assembly must satisfy structural, temporal, pedagogical, and blueprint constraints: exact marks and duration, section-wise counts, cognitive-level balance (Bloom's taxonomy~\cite{bloom1956,anderson2001}), chapter coverage, and avoidance of duplicate or near-duplicate questions. India's National Education Policy~\cite{moenep2020} further emphasizes competency-based assessment, making blueprint compliance a first-class engineering requirement.

\subsection{The Feasibility Gap}
Most production systems treat generation as a Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP) and employ SAT or CP solvers configured to terminate at the \emph{first feasible solution} ($B0$). While computationally efficient, first-feasible solving exhibits a major flaw: \textbf{a feasible paper is not necessarily a high-quality paper}.

Consider a paper requiring $M{=}10$ marks and $K{=}4$ questions with a target difficulty mix of 2 Easy and 2 Medium items. In a compatible item pool, a baseline first-feasible solver ($B0$) can halt at a valid assignment whose difficulty histogram differs from the target, provided it satisfies the marks, time, count, eligibility, and conflict constraints. An optimized solver ($B1$) instead balances the items to match the target difficulty histogram while maximizing chapter coverage. The gap between an unaligned feasible paper and a blueprint-aligned paper is the \emph{feasibility gap} addressed in this paper. In the present manifest, the selected-paper rules make chapter coverage nearly saturated; Pass~2 is therefore retained as a policy component but is not a differentiating empirical outcome.

\subsection{Research Questions and Scope}
This work investigates four research questions for a sequential generation setting:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Structural Alignment):} How much does multi-objective optimization improve metadata-histogram alignment over first-feasible generation ($B0$), with chapter coverage reported as a saturation check?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Solver Runtime):} What solver-runtime overhead does optimization introduce as item pools scale to $N=10{,}000$, excluding pre-solver graph construction?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Blueprint Tightness):} How does optimization gain behave as the feasible region contracts under tighter chapter-eligibility restrictions?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Soft Compliance):} To what extent does explicit multi-pass optimization improve soft blueprint compliance metrics over first-feasible generation?
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Scope and Boundaries.} (1) Pre-solver near-duplicate detection constructs a conflict graph $S$ that supplies pairwise at-most-one constraints; duplicate detection is a pipeline boundary condition, not an in-solver objective. (2) Production paper assembly solves template sections sequentially, carrying selected items forward. (3) The evaluation measures structural blueprint alignment and solver-only runtime (model construction and solving); embedding and graph construction costs are excluded, and psychometric IRT validity is not evaluated.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%===============================================================================

\subsection{Automated Test Assembly}
Automated Test Assembly (ATA) is a mature field in psychometrics and operations research. Foundational work~\cite{theunissen1985,vanderLinden2005,vanderLinden1989,lord1980} formulates assembly as 0--1 integer programming, often maximizing test information subject to content constraints. Recent reviews cover parallel-form and metaheuristic approaches~\cite{ignjatovic2021}, while cognitive-diagnosis and multiobjective formulations extend the setting~\cite{li2021,nguyen2021}. In contrast, this study uses a non-IRT, metadata-only regime with marks, time, ordinal difficulty, chapter, and cognitive labels. Multiobjective CP distinguishes scalarization from explicit priority search~\cite{bennetto2021,ehrgott2005}; we use that distinction to evaluate policies rather than claim a new CP method.

\subsection{Constraint Optimization Primitives}
Modern CP solvers using SAT and CDCL techniques, such as Google OR-Tools CP-SAT~\cite{perron2023,nieuwenhuis2006}, efficiently handle Boolean selection, linear bounds, and pairwise conflicts. Multi-criteria optimization can use weighted scalarization or multi-pass lexicographic sequences~\cite{ehrgott2005,ignizio1976}.

\section{Problem Formalization}
\label{sec:formalization}
%===============================================================================

Let $Q=\{q_1,\dots,q_n\}$ be an item bank. Each item $q_i=(m_i,t_i,T_i,d_i,b_i)$ has marks $m_i\in\mathbb{Z}^+$, completion time $t_i\in\mathbb{Z}^+$, topic tags $T_i\subseteq\mathcal{T}$, difficulty band $d_i\in\{1,\dots,|D|\}$, and Bloom's cognitive level $b_i\in\mathcal{B}$. An eligibility set $E\subseteq\mathcal{T}$ filters candidate items: only items satisfying $T_i\cap E\neq\emptyset$ are included in the solver pool. A required-coverage set $R\subseteq\mathcal{T}$ specifies chapters to be represented. In this paper, \emph{chapter coverage} is the instantiated dataset metric $\sum_{t\in R}y_t$, i.e., the number of required chapters represented by at least one selected item; the term is used consistently for this quantity.

A blueprint is specified as $B=(M,W,K,E,R,\Delta,\mathcal{H},\mathcal{S})$, where $M$ is total marks, $W$ is duration, $K$ is total question count, $\Delta=(\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_{|D|})$ is the target difficulty distribution ($\sum_k\Delta_k=K$), and $\mathcal{H}$ and $\mathcal{S}$ represent hard and soft blueprint constraints.

\subsection{Decision Variables and Baseline $B0$}
Let binary decision variable $x_i\in\{0,1\}$ represent item selection ($x_i=1$ iff $q_i$ is selected). The baseline first-feasible solver ($B0$) finds $x\in\{0,1\}^n$ satisfying:
\begin{align}
\sum_{i=1}^n m_i x_i &= M \quad &&\text{(Exact total marks)} \label{eq:marks} \\
\sum_{i=1}^n t_i x_i &= W \quad &&\text{(Exact total duration)} \label{eq:time} \\
\sum_{i=1}^n x_i &= K \quad &&\text{(Exact question count)} \label{eq:count} \\
x_i + x_j &\le 1 \quad \forall (i,j)\in S \quad &&\text{(Anti-duplicate conflicts)} \label{eq:conflict} \\
x_i = 1 &\Rightarrow T_i\cap E\neq\emptyset \quad \forall i \quad &&\text{(Topic eligibility)} \label{eq:eligibility}
\end{align}
where $S\subseteq Q\times Q$ is the pre-computed set of conflicting near-duplicate pairs. Required chapter coverage is modeled using binary indicators $y_t\in\{0,1\}$ for $t\in R$:
\begin{equation}
y_t \le \sum_{i:\,t\in T_i} x_i \quad \forall t\in R \label{eq:coverage_link}
\end{equation}
For policies that optimize coverage, this upper-bound linking is sufficient because maximizing $y_t$ forces $y_t=1$ whenever chapter $t$ is represented. In the reported manifest, coverage is an objective indicator rather than a separate hard requirement; it is not imposed as a mandatory lower bound. For policies that do not optimize coverage, reported coverage is computed directly from the selected item topics rather than from unconstrained $y_t$ values. The baseline $B0$ has no objective function; it halts at the first assignment satisfying the hard constraints.

\subsection{Algorithmic Properties}
For one section with $n_s$ eligible tier-matched items, $e_s$ conflict edges, and $h_s$ additional hard blueprint rules, the base selection model has $n_s$ item-selection variables and three aggregate equalities, plus $e_s$ conflict inequalities and the additional rules. Eligibility is a domain pre-filter, not an additional solver constraint. In the seven-band, three-Bloom-group implementation, $B1$ adds seven difficulty-deviation variables, $|R|$ coverage indicators, and three Bloom-deviation variables; the absolute-value linearizations contribute two inequalities per difficulty band and two per Bloom group, while coverage contributes $|R|$ linking inequalities. These counts exclude whole-paper cross-section constraints and should be read as model-size components rather than a complete count for every policy. The explicit conflict-constraint count is therefore linear in $e_s$ after graph construction, although solve time need not be linear and constructing $S$ is outside this solver model. The three-pass policy invokes CP-SAT up to three times per section, each under the 30\,s cap; it is a policy layer on the same selection model, not a new solver algorithm.

The feasibility problem is NP-hard because cardinality-constrained subset sum is a special case: assign item marks to the input integers, fix count to the required cardinality, set all times to one with $W=K$, and omit conflicts and optional rules. The exact-marks constraint then asks whether a size-$K$ subset sums to $M$~\cite{garey1979}. The count--time dynamic programme used by $B_{LS}$ is therefore pseudo-polynomial in the integer time bound, not a polynomial-time solution to the general feasibility problem.

\section{Policy Formulations and Optimization Models}
\label{sec:proposed}
%===============================================================================

We evaluate policy implementations rather than a new solver algorithm. The core comparison contrasts first-feasible search with an independent constructive--local baseline, two single-objective controls, and sequential and joint lexicographic CP-SAT policies under the same blueprint constraints.

\subsection{Bounded Lexicographic CP-SAT Policy ($B1$)}
$B1$ implements a three-pass lexicographic CP-SAT policy. When every pass returns \texttt{OPTIMAL}, it realizes the stated priority order; a time-capped \texttt{FEASIBLE} pass instead propagates an incumbent bound and provides only a bounded-time approximation:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Pass 1 (Primary Difficulty Alignment):} Minimize $L_1$ difficulty deviation $D(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{|D|} u_k$, where auxiliary integer variables $u_k \ge 0$ linearize the absolute error:
    \begin{equation}
    u_k \ge \sum_{i:\,d_i=k} x_i - \Delta_k \quad \text{and} \quad u_k \ge \Delta_k - \sum_{i:\,d_i=k} x_i \quad \forall k \label{eq:u_bounds}
    \end{equation}
    When Pass~1 is \texttt{OPTIMAL}, let $D^*$ be its optimal deviation; for a time-capped \texttt{FEASIBLE} result, $D^*$ denotes the incumbent deviation.
    \item \textbf{Pass 2 (Secondary Chapter Coverage):} Constrain $\sum_k u_k \le D^*$ and maximize required chapter coverage $\sum_{t\in R} y_t$. When Pass~2 is \texttt{OPTIMAL}, let $C^*$ be its optimal coverage score; for a time-capped \texttt{FEASIBLE} result, $C^*$ denotes the incumbent coverage score.
    \item \textbf{Pass 3 (Tertiary Soft Compliance):} Constrain $\sum_k u_k \le D^*$ and $\sum_{t\in R} y_t = C^*$, and minimize Bloom-level deviation $P(x) = \sum_{g\in\mathcal{B}} | \sum_{i:b_i\in g} x_i - T_g |$, where $T_g$ is the target count for cognitive group $g$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Three-pass lexicographic CP-SAT solver ($B1$)}
\label{alg:threepass}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Item bank $Q$; blueprint parameters; conflict graph $S$
\Ensure Hard-constraint-valid selection $x^*$, or INFEASIBLE/UNKNOWN
\State Construct base model $\mathcal{M}$ with constraints \eqref{eq:marks}--\eqref{eq:eligibility}
\State Add auxiliary variables $u_k$ and linearized constraints \eqref{eq:u_bounds}; set objective $\min \sum_k u_k$
\State $st_1 \gets \textsc{Solve}(\mathcal{M})$
\If{$st_1 = \text{INFEASIBLE}$} \Return INFEASIBLE \EndIf
\If{$st_1 \notin \{\text{OPTIMAL}, \text{FEASIBLE}\}$} \Return UNKNOWN \EndIf
\State $D^* \gets \text{incumbent Pass-1 objective value}$; add constraint $\sum_k u_k \le D^*$
\State Add chapter indicators $y_t$ and linking constraints \eqref{eq:coverage_link}; set objective $\max \sum_{t\in R} y_t$
\State $st_2 \gets \textsc{Solve}(\mathcal{M})$
\If{$st_2 = \text{INFEASIBLE}$} \Return INFEASIBLE \EndIf
\If{$st_2 \notin \{\text{OPTIMAL}, \text{FEASIBLE}\}$} \Return UNKNOWN \EndIf
\State $C^* \gets \text{incumbent Pass-2 objective value}$; add constraint $\sum_{t\in R} y_t = C^*$
\State Add Bloom deviation linearizations; set objective $\min P(x)$
\State $st_3 \gets \textsc{Solve}(\mathcal{M})$
\If{$st_3 \in \{\text{OPTIMAL}, \text{FEASIBLE}\}$} \Return $x^*$ \EndIf
\If{$st_3 = \text{INFEASIBLE}$} \Return INFEASIBLE \EndIf
\Return UNKNOWN
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent\textbf{Status handling.} The 30\,s solver budget is a defensive safeguard; all feasible RQ1 and RQ4 runs completed well under 2.0\,s. The budget accepts both \texttt{OPTIMAL} and \texttt{FEASIBLE}: a time-capped feasible result satisfies the hard constraints but does not certify global optimality. Carrying such an incumbent into the next pass makes the bounded-time procedure an approximation to certified lexicographic optimization; all reported RQ1 common-feasible B1 passes were nevertheless recorded as \texttt{OPTIMAL} in the supplied status audit. In the 70 common-feasible RQ1 papers, all 350 sections completed all three B1 passes: 1,050/1,050 statuses were \texttt{OPTIMAL} (artifact: \texttt{results\_b1\_status\_audit.json}).

\subsection{Alternative Baseline and Control Policies}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{$B_{LS}$ (Constructive--Local Baseline):} A non-CP-SAT constructive dynamic programme selects an exact count--time seed using greedy metadata priorities, followed by deterministic improving equal-time swaps. The dynamic programme enforces hard feasibility; its scoring and local search do not optimize the CP-SAT objectives.
    \item \textbf{$B2$ (Weighted-Sum Scalarization):} Solves a single CP-SAT pass minimizing $\alpha D(x) + \beta U(x) + \gamma P(x)$, where $U(x) = |R| - \sum_{t\in R} y_t$. The default shared-suite weight vector is $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(1,1,1)$, using raw rather than normalized objective components; results should therefore be interpreted as specific to this policy scale.
    \item \textbf{$B_C$ (Direct Compliance Control):} A joint whole-paper CP-SAT model minimizes $D_{\mathrm{total}}+P_{\mathrm{paper}}$, the affine equivalent of the reported complete-paper soft-compliance loss because every template paper has 38 questions. It is a single-objective control, not a new optimization method.
    \item \textbf{$B_J$ (Joint Lexicographic Control):} A whole-paper CP-SAT model applies the same $D\!\to\!C\!\to\!P$ priority order as $B1$, while enforcing all section constraints and cross-section uniqueness simultaneously.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Bounded Lookahead-Guided Sequential Diagnostic}
To test whether sequential lock-in can be reduced without replacing the production decomposition, we add a bounded lookahead policy. At each section, the policy generates up to $k=3$ distinct B1 candidates with $\varepsilon=0$ tolerance on the three lexicographic objectives. For each candidate it removes the selected IDs and scores the remaining sections using a fixed, deliberately coarse relaxed estimator. For a remaining section $s$, let $r_s$ be the number of unfilled item slots and $w_s$ the remaining required time. Define the binary reachability term
\begin{equation}
R_s = \mathbf{1}\left\{\begin{array}{l}
\exists\text{ an eligibility-, exclusion-, and conflict-consistent relaxed}\\[-1mm]
\text{count--time state reaching }(r_s,w_s)
\end{array}\right\},
\end{equation}
and let $E_s$, $D_s$, $B_s$, and $G_s$ denote, respectively, the number of eligible candidates, candidates in target difficulty bands, candidates in target Bloom groups, and candidate conflict incidences after removing the items already committed; $G_s$ is a conflict penalty, not a support term. We normalize each count by the corresponding maximum over the candidate set for that decision point, with a zero denominator mapped to zero:
\begin{equation}
\hat E_s=E_s/E_s^{\max},\quad \hat D_s=D_s/D_s^{\max},\quad \hat B_s=B_s/B_s^{\max},\quad \hat G_s=G_s/G_s^{\max}.
\end{equation}
The section score is
\begin{equation}
L_s = 100(2R_s-1)+\hat E_s+\hat D_s+\hat B_s-\hat G_s,\qquad L=\sum_{s\in\text{remaining}}L_s.
\end{equation}
Thus the reachability term lies in $\{-100,+100\}$, whereas the three normalized support terms each lie in $[0,1]$ and the normalized conflict penalty lies in $[0,1]$. The aggregate non-reachability contribution therefore lies in $[-1,3]$. The $100$ scale is intentional: it makes the coarse reachability signal dominate small differences in pool support and conflict penalty, while still allowing those terms to rank candidates when the reachability status is tied. The score is a local ranking heuristic, not calibrated as a probability or a comparable quality measure across different section counts; comparisons are therefore made only among candidates at the same decision point, with the same set of remaining sections.

The estimator is not a completion counter and does not certify future feasibility. The $+100/-100$ construction was chosen as a transparent diagnostic scale rather than fitted from outcomes. Because the lookahead policy generates up to three B1 candidates and evaluates downstream states repeatedly, it adds roughly a factor-of-two runtime overhead on this corpus without improving the observed quality metrics. It is therefore retained as a methodological ablation that tests the value of downstream awareness, not as a production recommendation. Each candidate-generation subsolve uses one worker, seed 1, and a 5\,s cap. This diagnostic is evaluated on the same 80-blueprint manifest and is treated as secondary evidence rather than a replacement for the pre-registered B1 policy.

\subsection{Constructive--Local Baseline Details}
For a section, $B_{LS}$ first filters the bank by eligibility, mark tier, and previously selected IDs. Its dynamic-programming state $F[c,w]$ stores the highest-priority ID tuple reaching count $c$ and time $w$, initialized as $F[0,0]=(\,0,\emptyset\,)$. The fixed per-item priority combines target difficulty and Bloom-group weights, chapter novelty, and a fixed per-blueprint deterministic jitter; exact score ties use lexicographically smaller ID tuples. Extending a state is allowed only when the count and time bounds hold and the candidate has no edge in $S$ to an item already in the tuple. Thus the seed respects exact count, duration, mark tier (and hence marks for this template), eligibility, cross-section exclusion, and supplied conflict edges. A missing $F[K,W]$ is reported as a heuristic failure, never as a certificate of infeasibility.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Constructive--local baseline ($B_{LS}$) for one section}
\label{alg:bls}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Eligible tier-matched pool $P$, excluded IDs $X$, conflict graph $S$, $(K,W)$
\State Filter $P\gets P\setminus X$; $F[0,0]\gets\emptyset$
\For{each $q\in P$ in fixture order}
  \For{each populated $(c,w)$ in descending count/time order}
    \If{$c<K$, $w+t_q\leq W$, and $q$ conflicts with no ID in $F[c,w]$}
      \State Update $F[c+1,w+t_q]$ if its priority score is larger; break ties by ID tuple
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\If{$F[K,W]$ is absent} \Return HEURISTIC\_FAILURE \EndIf
\State $x\gets F[K,W]$; repeatedly take the first ID-ordered equal-time swap that strictly improves $(D,P,-C)$ and preserves $S$
\State Stop after no improving swap or 100 accepted/rejected search rounds; \Return $x$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

With $n_s$ candidates, the construction has $O(n_s K W)$ count--time states without conflicts; tuple conflict checks add a factor $O(K)$ in the implementation. The bounded local phase examines at most 100 rounds of $O(K(n_s-K))$ swaps, each checked against at most $K-1$ selected IDs. This comparator is deliberately heuristic: unlike CP-SAT, it offers no optimality certificate.

\subsection{Compliance Metrics}
Blueprint compliance is partitioned into non-negotiable hard rules $\mathcal{H}$ (e.g., exact marks, time, question count) and soft distributional goals $\mathcal{S}$ (difficulty alignment and Bloom fit). For hard compliance, $CR_{\mathrm{hard}}(x) = 1.0$ for any valid output. Soft compliance is scored as:
\begin{equation}
CR_{\mathrm{soft}}(x) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \left[1 - \min\left(1, \frac{D(x)}{2K}\right)\right] + \left[1 - \min\left(1, \frac{P(x)}{2K}\right)\right] \right)
\end{equation}
For a section, $D=\sum_{k=1}^{7}|\sum_{i:d_i=k}x_i-\Delta_k|$ and $P=\sum_{g\in\{\mathrm{RU,APP,AEC}\}}|\sum_{i:b_i\in g}x_i-T_g|$; because both observed and target histograms sum to $K$, $0\leq D,P\leq2K$. For a complete template paper, $K_{\mathrm{tot}}=38$, $D_{\mathrm{total}}$ is the sum of section deviations, $P_{\mathrm{paper}}$ uses the 54/24/22 paper-level target, and $0\leq D_{\mathrm{total}},P_{\mathrm{paper}}\leq76$. Hence the clipping in $CR_{\mathrm{soft}}$ is inactive here and $CR_{\mathrm{soft}}=1-(D_{\mathrm{total}}+P_{\mathrm{paper}})/(4K_{\mathrm{tot}})$. For $B2$, $U=|R|-\sum_{t\in R}y_t\in[0,7]$ and the fixed raw objective is $D+U+P$; its unnormalized $(1,1,1)$ weights were chosen before all runs as a concrete scalar policy, not tuned from the sensitivity grid. It is therefore a different preference model from lexicographic $B1$, not a direct test of $B1$'s priority semantics.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}
%===============================================================================

\subsection{Dataset and Benchmark Setup}
The empirical corpus consists of \textbf{475 CBSE Class X Mathematics items} retrospectively extracted from a production assessment system. Every item contains populated metadata: marks (1--5), completion time, ordinal difficulty (bands 1--7), Bloom cognitive tag, and chapter classifications. The study does not evaluate a live deployment, user adoption, or operational outcomes. The bank is easy-skewed (83\% of items in bands 1--3) and marks-skewed (73\% 1-mark items), providing a production-derived testbed for difficulty alignment. For solver scaling experiments (RQ2), synthetic pools calibrated to the empirical marginal distributions are generated up to $N{=}10{,}000$; conflict density and feature correlations are not claimed to reproduce the production bank.

\subsection{Blueprint Manifest Generation}
For RQ1, 80 blueprints are deterministically generated using a fixed random seed (\texttt{random.Random(42)}). For each ID in ascending order, 5--7 eligible chapters are sampled uniformly from the sorted chapter list; target difficulty counts are normalized draws weighted $(4,5,4,3,2,1,0.3)$ with cyclic remainder assignment, and Bloom targets use the 54/24/22 split. Each blueprint specifies five template sections (A--E), containing 38 questions in total, with section-level question counts, marks, time bounds, and target difficulty distributions. The fixed structural parameters used for every manifest blueprint are shown in Table~\ref{tab:manifest}; the seed-42 target histograms are recorded in the blueprint manifest and regeneration artifacts in the reproducibility package. Sections are solved sequentially in order A$\to$E, carrying selected item IDs forward to enforce cross-section uniqueness.

\subsection{Whole-Paper Control on Excluded Blueprints}
To resolve whether the 10 blueprints yielding an \texttt{INFEASIBLE} status under sequential section solving were caused by greedy section lock-in or underlying blueprint unsatisfiability, a whole-paper joint CP-SAT model was formulated. The joint model pools constraints \eqref{eq:marks}--\eqref{eq:eligibility} across all sections simultaneously. The supplied joint-model audit reports all 10 excluded blueprints as \texttt{INFEASIBLE} under the same modeled hard constraints. This supports the interpretation that the 10 infeasible runs represent global blueprint infeasibility rather than an artifact of sequential policy continuation; the audit files are required to independently verify this result. The common-feasible baseline count is therefore exactly 70/80.

\noindent\textbf{RQ1 (Structural Alignment).} Table~\ref{tab:rq1} replaces a weak first-feasible-only comparison with independent and whole-paper controls. On the fixed manifest, $B1$ improves $D$ over $B0$ in all 70 pairs (median difference 12, 95\% CI $[10,12]$, Holm-adjusted $p=3.6{\times}10^{-12}$, $r=0.87$). These are paired results for the tested manifest, not independent evidence from 70 unrelated experiments. Against $B_{LS}$, $B1$ improves $D$ in 22 pairs and ties in 48 (median difference 0, CI $[0,0]$, adjusted $p=1.4{\times}10^{-5}$, $r=0.56$). This establishes structural-alignment differences only, not psychometric or pedagogical superiority.

The direct-compliance control $B_C$ and weighted $B2$ match $B1$'s median difficulty while achieving lower median Bloom deviation (22 versus 24) and lower solver runtime (0.04 and 0.07 versus 0.13\,s). Because the theoretical bounds make clipping inactive for every feasible paper, and every complete template paper contains 38 questions, $CR_{\mathrm{soft}}=1-(D_{\mathrm{total}}+P_{\mathrm{paper}})/(4K_{\mathrm{tot}})$ differs from minimizing $D_{\mathrm{total}}+P_{\mathrm{paper}}$ only by a positive affine transformation; this makes $B_C$ affine-equivalent to the reported complete-paper loss, but not equivalent to $B1$, whose lexicographic $D\to C\to P$ preference protects each priority level sequentially rather than trading the components in a single sum. Thus $B1$ is not presented as the raw quality--runtime optimum: its contribution is a deterministic, weight-free specification of priority order. Coverage has limited discriminating power in this template: all paired core-policy comparisons tie in represented-chapter count, despite a per-policy IQR of [6,7] over seven possible chapters. It is therefore not evidence that Pass~2 improves quality.

The bounded lookahead diagnostic ties sequential $B1$ on difficulty, Bloom deviation, and coverage across all 70 comparable fixed-manifest papers, while increasing median solver runtime from $0.127$ to $0.259$\,s (an increase of $0.132$\,s; lower runtime is better). It therefore provides a reproducible test of downstream-aware candidate selection, but no evidence of a quality gain on this corpus; the observed overhead makes it unsuitable as the default production policy here.

\noindent\textbf{RQ3 (Blueprint Tightness).} In a separate tightness sweep of 100 blueprint--tier cases in total (20 cases per tier), restricting eligible chapter counts from 7 down to 3, overall feasibility drops from 100\% (tier 7) to 25\% (tier 3). Conditional on feasible cases, $B1$ maintains a median difficulty-deviation improvement of $6$ to $12$ units over $B0$ across the tested tiers; these cases are treated as a sensitivity analysis rather than as independent replications.

\noindent\textbf{RQ4 (Soft Compliance).} Each pair consists of the B0 and B1 outputs for the same generated blueprint and eligibility tier; pairs are retained only when both policies return feasible outputs. Among 66 common-feasible blueprint pairs retained from the tightness sweep, using the complete-paper form of $CR_{\mathrm{soft}}$ rather than an average of section scores, $B1$ increases median soft compliance score from $0.408$ ($B0$) to $0.566$ and outperforms $B0$ in all 66 cases (Wilcoxon $p=1.6{\times}10^{-12}$, $r=0.87$). Because the analysis uses common-feasible pairs, these results should be interpreted together with the tier-wise feasibility counts rather than as a feasibility advantage.

%===============================================================================
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
%===============================================================================

\noindent\textbf{Policy Design Trade-Offs.} First-feasible generation leaves structural alignment uncaptured relative to optimized controls. Multi-pass $B1$ supplies an explicit, weight-free priority hierarchy: difficulty alignment first, chapter coverage second, and Bloom fit third. Its extra passes are therefore a methodological and governance choice rather than a runtime-optimal policy. On this corpus, $B1$ is about twice as slow as the fastest optimized controls and gains no lookahead quality, so a different latency budget may favor $B_C$ or $B2$.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} (1) Structural blueprint alignment does not imply psychometric validity; empirical item response curves require post-examination response data. (2) Scaling and policy runtimes cover model construction and CP-SAT solving only; $O(n^2)$ embedding generation and conflict-graph construction were not benchmarked. (3) The evaluation is based on one easy-skewed 475-item CBSE Class X Mathematics corpus and fixed template family; results may not generalize to other subjects, banks, or section designs. (4) Chapter coverage is weakly discriminating here, and the duplicate-detection stress test is constructed rather than human validated; consequently, the experiments do not establish the precision or recall of the upstream near-duplicate detector. (5) Difficulty and Bloom labels are source-system ordinal metadata; label noise, annotator agreement, and consistency of those labels were not evaluated.

%===============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%===============================================================================

This paper presented an applied algorithm-engineering study of policy-aware CP-SAT for sequential examination-paper assembly. Its contribution is a reproducible comparison of objective policies and sequential versus joint decomposition on a production-derived, metadata-only benchmark, rather than a new CP-SAT algorithm. Across the five tested manifests, sequential lexicographic $B1$ has manifest-level median difficulty deviations of 38--40 versus 50--52 for first-feasible $B0$; the controls show that its main demonstrated value is explicit priority semantics rather than universal raw dominance. Lookahead ties $B1$ on quality but doubles runtime on this corpus. A joint control and alternative-order study found no ordering loss on the evaluated corpus and template. These results concern structural alignment and solver runtime only; end-to-end latency, psychometric validity, and cross-corpus generalization remain future work.
